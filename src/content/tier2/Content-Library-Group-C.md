# Content Library — Group C: Pillar Diagnosis Blocks

> **The parenthetical ranges in these band headings are labels for writers, not the rule.**
> The engine compares with `<=`: `<= 40`, `<= 60`, `<= 75`, `<= 90`, else Band 5. Scores are DECIMAL, and an integer range such as `41–60` leaves gaps that 3.9% of answer sets fall through. See `CANONICAL-VALUES.md` §6 and Scoring Specification §5.

**35 pieces (7 pillars × 5 bands) · Blocks Sprint 9 · Target: Week 9 · CRITICAL PATH**

These are the heart of the ₹1,999 + GST report. Each block is what a founder reads under their pillar score.

**The boundary that must hold:** every block **diagnoses**. None **prescribes**. A block may say *"SOPs exist for some workflows but not consistently enough for a franchisee to operate without your input."* It must not say *"document your top five workflows over the next 30 days."* That sentence belongs in Tier 3's roadmap, and letting it drift here is what causes Tier 2 to cannibalise Tier 3.

**Structure of each block:** what this score looks like in practice → why it matters for franchising specifically → what the next level up looks like, described but not instructed.

Items marked **[CHECK]** rest on a pattern claim Corporate Culture should verify against real engagements.

# 1. UNIT ECONOMICS (20%)

### Band 1 — Not Yet (0–40)

> At this level, the numbers do not yet support a franchise offer. Either margins are too thin to survive a royalty on top, payback runs long enough that an investor would struggle to justify it against alternatives, or the underlying figures are not yet known with enough confidence to model. All three are common in businesses that are profitable but have never had to prove their economics to an outsider.
>
> This matters more than any other pillar because it is the one a franchise investor examines first and forgives least. A brand can have weak systems and still attract partners; a brand whose numbers do not work cannot, and the ones who sign anyway become the network's most difficult problem within eighteen months.
>
> Businesses that clear this level can state their outlet-level margin and payback with confidence, and those numbers leave room for a franchisee to earn a return after paying the brand.

### Band 2 — Early Stage (41–60)

> Your economics work for you but have not yet been tested as a franchise proposition. The gap is usually the same one: an outlet you own carries no franchise fee and pays no royalty, so the margin you see is not the margin a franchisee would see. Businesses at this level often have genuinely healthy company-owned performance and have not yet subtracted what the brand would take.
>
> For franchising specifically, this is the difference between a model that attracts partners and one that loses them. The first franchisee discovers the real numbers whether or not the brand modelled them in advance — and a franchisee who feels the economics were oversold rarely opens a second outlet.
>
> The next level up is a business that has modelled franchisee economics separately from its own, and knows what the offer looks like from the other side of the table.

### Band 3 — Emerging (61–75)

> The economics are broadly sound but carry a specific constraint — most often payback that runs longer than comparable formats, or margins that work at current scale but tighten once brand fees are layered on. Businesses here are franchisable, but the offer would benefit from structuring before it goes to market rather than after.
>
> This matters because franchise investors compare. An opportunity does not need to be the strongest in its category, but it does need to survive comparison with two or three alternatives the investor is looking at simultaneously — and payback is the number they compare first.
>
> Businesses at the next level have economics that hold up under that comparison without requiring explanation or optimistic assumptions.

### Band 4 — Ready (76–90)

> Your unit economics support a franchise offer. Margins leave room for both parties, payback sits within a range investors accept, and the underlying figures are known rather than estimated. This is the position from which fee and royalty structure can be set deliberately rather than defensively.
>
> What tends to matter at this stage is not whether the numbers work but whether they hold across locations. Economics proven in one city with known rents and established footfall behave differently in a market where the brand has no history.
>
> The strongest businesses in this range have seen their model perform in more than one market and know which variables move it.

### Band 5 — Scale-Ready (91–100)

> Your economics are a genuine asset in the franchise proposition rather than something to be defended. Margins are strong, payback is short enough to be a selling point, and the model has demonstrated it holds outside its home market.
>
> The risk at this level is rarely the numbers themselves. It is that strong economics attract volume — more enquiries, faster expansion, more partners than the support structure was built to carry — and a network's first failures usually trace to growth outpacing support rather than to the model.
>
> The businesses that scale well from here are the ones whose support infrastructure matches the demand their economics generate.

**[CHECK]** — Band 2 asserts that a franchisee who feels economics were oversold rarely opens a second outlet. Confirm this matches what you've seen; if the more common outcome is something else (early exit, renegotiation, disputes), that sentence should say so.

# 2. OPERATIONAL REPEATABILITY (20%)

### Band 1 — Not Yet (0–40)

> The business currently runs on knowledge held by people rather than knowledge written down. This is not unusual — most successful small businesses are built this way, and it is often why they are good. Staff who have been with you for years know how things are done, and that knowledge has never needed to leave the building.
>
> Franchising is the point at which it does need to leave the building. A franchisee three cities away cannot absorb what your team knows by working alongside them, and the gap between what is documented and what is actually required becomes their problem to solve — usually by inventing their own version.
>
> Businesses that clear this level have converted enough of that knowledge into written process that someone outside the original team could follow it.

### Band 2 — Early Stage (41–60)

> Some processes are documented and others are not, and the split usually falls the same way: routine operations are written down while the parts requiring judgement remain with you or a long-serving manager. What exists is real but incomplete.
>
> This matters for franchising because the undocumented parts are typically the ones that determine quality. A franchisee can follow an opening checklist; what they cannot follow is the unwritten standard for when something is good enough to serve, or how an unhappy customer is handled.
>
> The next level up is a business where documentation covers not just what is done routinely but how decisions are made when the routine breaks.

### Band 3 — Emerging (61–75)

> Documentation is substantially in place, with gaps that show up in specific areas rather than across the board — most commonly quality control, supplier management, or the handling of situations that fall outside normal operation. A new manager could run the business from what exists, though they would come to you for the edges.
>
> For franchising, those edges are where consistency breaks across a network. Each franchisee resolves them independently, and the brand discovers three different standards operating under one name.
>
> Businesses at the next level have closed those gaps and have a mechanism for noticing when standards drift rather than relying on the founder to spot it.

### Band 4 — Ready (76–90)

> Your systems are documented well enough to be transferred. A trained operator could reproduce your result without your presence, and there is a mechanism — audit, checklist, review — that catches drift before it becomes a pattern.
>
> What separates this level from the one above is usually consistency across sites rather than the quality of documentation itself. Systems that hold in one location under direct oversight behave differently when the oversight is remote.
>
> The strongest businesses in this range have already proven their systems hold across more than one location without the founder present.

### Band 5 — Scale-Ready (91–100)

> Your operational systems are genuinely transferable and have been proven so. Documentation is comprehensive, quality control is structured rather than instinctive, and the business does not depend on any individual's accumulated knowledge to maintain standards.
>
> This is the pillar that most often determines whether a network holds together at twenty outlets, and you are positioned well on it. The remaining risk is maintenance — systems documented once and never revised drift out of alignment with how the business actually operates, and the gap usually goes unnoticed until a new franchisee follows the written version.
>
> Businesses that sustain this level treat documentation as something maintained, not completed.

# 3. SUPPORT INFRASTRUCTURE (15%)

### Band 1 — Not Yet (0–40)

> There is currently no structured capacity to support a franchisee after signing. No defined training programme, no named person accountable for partner success, and no marketing or operational support a franchisee could expect as part of the offer.
>
> This matters because franchising is a support obligation, not a licensing transaction. The fee and royalty a franchisee pays are in exchange for something continuing — and a brand that cannot articulate what that something is will find the question asked pointedly during due diligence, and answered by absence afterwards.
>
> Businesses that clear this level have at minimum a defined training programme and someone whose responsibility includes franchisee performance rather than only company operations.

### Band 2 — Early Stage (41–60)

> Some support capability exists — usually informal training the founder delivers personally, and help given when a problem is raised rather than a structured programme. It works at one or two partners because the founder absorbs the load.
>
> The difficulty is that this model does not survive its own success. Support delivered personally by the founder caps the network at whatever the founder's time allows, and the constraint arrives suddenly rather than gradually — typically at the third or fourth partner.
>
> The next level up is support that exists independently of the founder's direct involvement: documented training, defined support commitments, and someone other than the founder accountable for delivering them.

### Band 3 — Emerging (61–75)

> Support structures exist but are not yet complete — commonly training is defined while ongoing support is reactive, or marketing support is offered without a clear scope of what a franchisee can expect. A partner joining now would be supported, though the terms of that support would be discovered rather than stated.
>
> For franchising, undefined support becomes a source of friction rather than failure. Franchisees form expectations from what they were told during the sale, and the gap between that and what is delivered is where most partner relationships sour.
>
> Businesses at the next level have support commitments defined explicitly enough that both sides know what was promised.

### Band 4 — Ready (76–90)

> You have real support infrastructure — training that can be delivered without you, defined ongoing support, and accountability for partner performance sitting with a named person or function. A franchisee joining now would know what they are receiving.
>
> What tends to matter from here is capacity rather than structure. Support that works for three partners strains at ten, and the strain shows first in responsiveness rather than in anything measurable.
>
> The strongest businesses in this range have thought about what their support model looks like at the network size they are targeting, not the size they are at.

### Band 5 — Scale-Ready (91–100)

> Your support infrastructure is a competitive strength. Training is structured and transferable, support commitments are explicit, performance monitoring exists, and the function does not depend on the founder's availability.
>
> This is the pillar that most directly determines franchisee renewal and referral — and a network where existing partners recommend the brand to others recruits far more cheaply than one relying on advertising.
>
> The businesses that sustain this level treat support capacity as something to expand ahead of network growth rather than in response to it.

# 4. FOUNDER & LEADERSHIP READINESS (15%)

### Band 1 — Not Yet (0–40)

> The business currently runs through you. Daily decisions come to you, your absence is noticeable, and the operation has not been structured to function independently. For a single successful outlet this is often the right arrangement — founder attention is frequently what makes a small business good.
>
> Franchising inverts it. A network multiplies the demands on a founder who is already the constraint, and the arrangement that works at one outlet becomes the reason the third one struggles. This pillar is also the hardest to fix quickly, because it requires the founder to change how they work rather than the business to change what it documents.
>
> Businesses that clear this level have started delegating decisions rather than tasks, and have someone other than the founder capable of running operations.

### Band 2 — Early Stage (41–60)

> Delegation has begun but is incomplete — typically tasks are delegated while decisions are not, or a manager runs operations while anything unusual still routes to you. There is a team, but the business has not yet been structured to run without you.
>
> This matters because franchise support is itself a demand on founder time, and it arrives on top of existing operational load. A founder who is still central to daily operations does not have the capacity to onboard and support partners, and that shortage tends to surface exactly when a new franchisee needs the most attention.
>
> The next level up is a business where operational decisions are made without the founder and reviewed afterwards rather than approved in advance.

### Band 3 — Emerging (61–75)

> The business operates substantially without you, with defined limits beyond which decisions still come back. This is a workable position — many franchisors operate this way — but the boundary matters. If the retained decisions are strategic, the arrangement scales. If they are operational, it does not.
>
> For franchising specifically, the question is whether your remaining involvement is the kind that multiplies across a network. Approving unusual purchases at one outlet is manageable; approving them at eight is not.
>
> Businesses at the next level have moved from controlling operations to overseeing outcomes.

### Band 4 — Ready (76–90)

> The business runs without your daily involvement, and your role has moved toward direction rather than operation. This is the position from which a founder can genuinely support a franchise network, because the capacity exists rather than having to be created.
>
> What matters at this level is usually willingness rather than capability — specifically, willingness to accept that a franchisee will run their outlet differently from how you would run it, provided the standards hold.
>
> The strongest businesses in this range have leadership capacity beyond the founder, so that partner support does not depend on one person's availability.

### Band 5 — Scale-Ready (91–100)

> The business is genuinely independent of your daily involvement, and there is leadership depth beyond you. This is the condition under which franchising works as intended — the founder's time goes to network development rather than being consumed by operations.
>
> The remaining consideration is not readiness but role. Founders who have successfully stepped back from operations sometimes step back into them when a franchisee struggles, and the network learns that intervention is available on request.
>
> Businesses that sustain this level support partners through structure rather than through the founder personally.

**[CHECK]** — Band 1 claims this pillar is the hardest to fix quickly because it requires the founder to change rather than the business to document. Confirm against your engagements; if founder-dependency actually resolves faster than, say, unit economics problems, this should be reworded.

# 5. MARKET READINESS (12%)

### Band 1 — Not Yet (0–40)

> Demand has been demonstrated in one place and not yet beyond it. The business may be performing well, but its performance is currently inseparable from its location, its existing customer relationships, and the founder's presence in that market.
>
> This matters for franchising because a franchisee is buying the assumption that the model travels. If it has not been tested outside its home ground, that assumption is unproven — and the franchisee, not the brand, carries the cost of finding out.
>
> Businesses that clear this level have evidence the model performs somewhere the brand was not already known.

### Band 2 — Early Stage (41–60)

> There is some evidence of demand beyond the original location, but not enough to establish a pattern — commonly a second outlet in the same city, or demand inferred from enquiries rather than demonstrated by trading. Site selection is still done by judgement rather than criteria.
>
> The difficulty this creates is in territory planning. A brand that cannot describe what makes a good location will struggle to assess whether a franchisee's proposed site will work, and site selection is one of the few franchise decisions that cannot be corrected afterwards.
>
> The next level up is a business with defined site criteria drawn from evidence rather than instinct.

### Band 3 — Emerging (61–75)

> The model has been shown to work in more than one setting, and there is a working sense of what makes a location viable. What is usually missing is the sequencing — which markets to enter in what order, and why.
>
> This matters because franchise expansion is frequently driven by where an investor happens to be rather than where the brand should be. Without a geographic logic, a network becomes scattered, support becomes expensive, and the brand builds no density anywhere.
>
> Businesses at the next level have an expansion sequence they can defend rather than a willingness to open wherever interest appears.

### Band 4 — Ready (76–90)

> Demand is validated across multiple markets, site criteria are defined, and there is a rationale for expansion sequencing. This is the position from which territory can be structured deliberately.
>
> What tends to matter from here is discipline rather than knowledge. Brands with good expansion logic still deviate from it when a capable investor appears in the wrong city, and the exceptions accumulate.
>
> The strongest businesses in this range hold their sequence even when it means declining an otherwise attractive partner.

### Band 5 — Scale-Ready (91–100)

> Market readiness is a strength. Demand is proven across markets, site criteria are evidence-based, and expansion sequencing is deliberate. The brand knows where it should go and in what order.
>
> The consideration at this level is usually pace against support capacity rather than market opportunity. A brand that can identify twenty viable markets is not necessarily a brand that can support twenty outlets.
>
> Businesses that scale well from here match expansion pace to support infrastructure rather than to market availability.

# 6. BRAND POSITIONING STRENGTH (10%)

### Band 1 — Not Yet (0–40)

> The brand is not yet doing work in the franchise proposition. There is little inbound interest, limited public visibility or review presence, and what the brand stands for has not been articulated in a form anyone outside the business would encounter.
>
> This matters because a franchise investor is choosing between starting something themselves and buying into a brand. The brand has to be worth the fee — and if a prospective partner cannot see evidence that the name carries weight with customers, the value of licensing it is unclear.
>
> Businesses that clear this level have some demonstrable market presence: reviews, recognition, or enquiries arriving without being solicited.

### Band 2 — Early Stage (41–60)

> There is a real brand with real customers, but its presence is local and largely undocumented. Customers value it; that value has not been made visible to anyone who has not experienced it directly.
>
> For franchising, this creates a specific problem during due diligence. An investor evaluating the opportunity will look for external evidence — reviews, coverage, search presence — and finding little, will discount claims about brand strength regardless of whether those claims are true.
>
> The next level up is a brand whose reputation is externally visible rather than only locally known.

### Band 3 — Emerging (61–75)

> The brand has genuine market presence and some inbound pull, but it is not yet distinct enough or consistent enough to be the primary reason a partner signs. Positioning exists but is not systematically expressed.
>
> This matters most in categories where investors have many options. A franchise investor comparing three brands in the same segment will weight brand differentiation heavily, because it is the part of the offer they cannot replicate independently.
>
> Businesses at the next level have a brand promise that is documented, consistently delivered, and evident to someone encountering the brand for the first time.

### Band 4 — Ready (76–90)

> The brand is an asset in the proposition. There is inbound interest, visible reputation, and a promise that is defined and consistently delivered. A prospective franchisee can see why licensing the name is worth paying for.
>
> What matters from here is protecting it. Brand strength built over years is diluted quickly by partners operating below standard, and the dilution is usually invisible to the brand until a review pattern shifts.
>
> The strongest businesses in this range have a mechanism for holding partners to the brand promise, not only to operational standards.

### Band 5 — Scale-Ready (91–100)

> The brand generates genuine investor pull — inbound enquiry, strong visible reputation, and a clearly expressed position in its category. This is a substantial advantage in partner recruitment, because it lowers acquisition cost and improves the quality of who applies.
>
> The risk is that strong brands attract partners motivated by the brand rather than by the business. An investor who signs because the name is well known, without examining whether they suit the operating model, becomes a difficult partner regardless of the brand's strength.
>
> Businesses that sustain this level screen as carefully when demand is high as when it is scarce.

# 7. PARTNER PROFILE DEFINITION (8%)

### Band 1 — Not Yet (0–40)

> There is not yet a defined view of who the right franchisee is. No stated criteria for capital, experience, or involvement, and no process by which a prospective partner would be assessed beyond conversation.
>
> This matters more than its weight suggests. Franchise partner selection is one of the few decisions in this list that cannot be corrected — a poorly matched partner cannot be removed easily, occupies a territory, and represents the brand to customers who cannot distinguish between the franchisee and the network.
>
> Businesses that clear this level have at least articulated what kind of partner they are looking for, even if the process for finding them is not yet built.

### Band 2 — Early Stage (41–60)

> There is a rough sense of the right partner — usually expressed as capital requirement and a general preference about involvement — but it is not documented and no structured assessment exists. Selection would be a judgement made in conversation.
>
> The difficulty is that judgement made in conversation is heavily influenced by enthusiasm and available capital, which are poor predictors of franchisee success. Brands consistently report that their most difficult partners were persuasive during selection.
>
> The next level up is defined criteria applied consistently rather than assessed case by case.

### Band 3 — Emerging (61–75)

> Partner criteria are defined, though the assessment process is not yet structured — commonly the brand knows what it is looking for but evaluates candidates informally, or criteria exist for capital and experience but not for operating fit.
>
> For franchising, the gap usually shows in the first difficult partner. Criteria that were never applied consistently are hard to invoke afterwards, and a brand that accepted one partner outside its stated profile finds it difficult to decline the next.
>
> Businesses at the next level assess partners against defined criteria consistently, including when it means declining someone who is otherwise attractive.

### Band 4 — Ready (76–90)

> You know who your right partner is and have a process for assessing whether a candidate matches. Criteria cover capital, experience and operating fit, and the assessment is applied rather than assumed.
>
> What matters from here is holding the line under pressure. Partner criteria are easiest to apply when there is a queue of candidates and hardest when a territory has been open for months.
>
> The strongest businesses in this range treat their partner profile as a constraint they keep rather than a preference they hold.

### Band 5 — Scale-Ready (91–100)

> Partner definition is a genuine strength — clear criteria, structured assessment, and a documented picture of what a successful franchisee in this system looks like. This is unusual, and it compounds: brands that select well have fewer struggling partners, which reduces support load and improves network performance.
>
> The consideration at this level is keeping the profile current. The partner who suits a five-outlet network is not always the one who suits a fifty-outlet network, and criteria set early can outlive their accuracy.
>
> Businesses that sustain this level revisit their partner profile as the network matures.

**[CHECK]** — Band 2 claims brands consistently report that their most difficult partners were persuasive during selection. This is a strong, specific claim. If your engagement experience supports it, it is one of the most useful sentences in the whole library. If not, replace it with the pattern you have actually observed.

## Notes for the Team

**Where these need your experience most.** The structure and voice are set. What will make them worth ₹1,999 + GST rather than generic is the substitution of real observed patterns for the general claims currently in place — specifically anywhere a block says "commonly," "usually," or "brands consistently report." Each of those is an invitation to replace a reasonable inference with something you have actually watched happen.

**The three CHECK items are the highest-value edits.** Each is a specific causal claim that would be memorable if true and damaging if a founder knows it to be false.

**On the "what better looks like" paragraph.** Every block closes by describing the next band up without instructing how to reach it. If an edit makes one of these read as advice, it has crossed into Tier 3's territory — the test is whether the sentence describes a *state* or an *action*.

**Bands 1 and 5 are written to be selectable without insult or flattery.** A founder scoring in Band 1 should feel accurately described rather than dismissed; a founder in Band 5 should find a real risk named rather than congratulation. If an edit softens Band 1 or inflates Band 5, the instrument loses its ends.
