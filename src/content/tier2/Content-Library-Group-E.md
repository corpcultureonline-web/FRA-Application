## E1. Report Introduction

*Opens the Tier 2 report, above the score.*

> This report is based on the {n_total} questions you answered about your business, assessed against seven pillars that determine whether a business can be franchised successfully.
>
> It is a diagnosis. It tells you where you stand on each pillar, which gaps cost you most, and what those gaps mean in practice. It does not tell you what to do about them — that is a separate exercise, and doing it properly requires financial detail this assessment did not ask for.
>
> Nothing here is a judgement on your business as a business. Franchise readiness measures something narrower: whether what you have built can be reproduced by someone else, in a place you are not, to a standard you would recognise.

## E2. Score Explanation

*Sits immediately below the headline score.*

> Your Franchise Readiness Score combines seven pillars, each weighted by how much it determines franchise success rather than business success generally.
>
> | Pillar | Weight |
> |---|---|
> | Unit Economics | 20% |
> | Operational Repeatability | 20% |
> | Support Infrastructure | 15% |
> | Founder & Leadership Readiness | 15% |
> | Market Readiness | 12% |
> | Brand Positioning Strength | 10% |
> | Partner Profile Definition | 8% |
>
> Unit Economics and Operational Repeatability carry the most weight because they are the two things a franchisee is actually buying: a model whose numbers work, and a system they can reproduce. A business can be strong elsewhere and still fail to franchise if either of those is missing.
>
> Legal and IP readiness is assessed separately rather than scored. It functions as a gate — some findings there cap your result regardless of how the seven pillars perform, because they are conditions rather than weaknesses.
>
> **This score is final.** It was calculated from your responses and does not change as you complete further assessments.

## E3. Ranked Gap Explanation

*Sits directly above the ranked gap list. This is the piece that prevents the ordering from looking wrong.*

> **These gaps are ordered by what they cost you, not by which score is lowest.**
>
> A weakness in a pillar carrying 20% of the assessment holds you back more than a larger weakness in one carrying 8%. So your lowest score may not appear first — what appears first is whatever is doing the most damage to your overall readiness.
>
> Fixing them in this order recovers the most ground for the least effort.

**Note on the last line:** it says "fixing them in this order" without saying how to fix anything — the ordering is information, not instruction. This is the closest Group E comes to the Tier 2 boundary, and it stays on the right side of it. If an edit turns this into guidance about *what to do first*, it has crossed into Tier 3.

## E4. Non-Negotiables Framing

*Introduces the legal and IP findings, shown separately from the score.*

> **Non-negotiables**
>
> These are separate from your score because they are not weaknesses to be improved — they are conditions to be met. A business can score well on all seven pillars and still be unable to franchise until these are resolved.
>
> They are listed here whether or not they affected your band, because each one will surface during an investor's due diligence.

## E5. What This Report Does Not Include

*Stated openly, per the design principle. Placed before the upgrade framing.*

> **What this report does not include**
>
> This assessment diagnoses. It deliberately stops short of five things:
>
> - **A franchisee economics model** — what a partner would actually earn after paying your fee and royalty, and how long their money takes to come back
> - **Fee and royalty benchmarking** — how your proposed structure compares to comparable formats in your category
> - **A franchise model recommendation** — which operating structure suits your business, and why
> - **A correction roadmap** — what to address, in what order, and within what timeframe
> - **Territory logic** — which markets to enter, in what sequence, with what exclusivity
>
> These are excluded because doing them properly requires financial and strategic detail this assessment did not ask for — franchisee investment, fee structure, royalty, revenue, and your own intentions for the business over the next three years.

**Why this is stated rather than left implicit:** a founder who discovers the omission themselves feels something was withheld. A founder told upfront reads the same fact as scope. This is the design principle applied to copy — Tier 2 withholds prescription openly rather than quietly.

## E6. Tier 3 Upgrade Framing

*Follows E5 directly.*

> **The Franchise Readiness Roadmap — ₹5,999 + 18% GST**
>
> The Roadmap picks up where this report stops. It asks seventeen further questions — most of them financial — and produces the five things listed above.
>
> The centre of it is a franchisee economics model: your investment, fee, royalty and revenue figures worked through to show what a partner actually earns and how long their payback runs. That calculation is rated separately from your Readiness Score, and the two frequently disagree — a business can be broadly ready to franchise while the specific deal it is offering does not pay back fast enough to attract good partners. Knowing which of those you are is usually worth more than the score itself.
>
> The Roadmap is ₹5,999 + 18% GST, and it is reviewed by an analyst before it reaches you.

**Note:** this frames the upgrade around the Risk Check specifically, because that is Tier 3's genuinely distinctive output. Listing five deliverables is a feature list; naming the one finding a founder cannot get anywhere else is a reason.

## E7. Category Positioning Statement — Template

*Qualitative comparison, drawn from real deal patterns. Not a percentile.*

**Structure to follow:**

> Among [category] brands at a comparable stage, [observation about where this business sits]. [What that typically means for the franchising path.]

**Worked examples:**

> Among F&B brands at a comparable stage, most have documented their core operating procedures before opening a second location. You are expanding ahead of that curve, which is not unusual — but it means the documentation work sits ahead of you rather than behind you.

> Among fashion retail brands in this investment range, franchise fees typically sit between ₹X and ₹Y with royalties in the Z% range. Your proposed structure sits above that on both counts, which does not make it wrong — but it does mean the offer has to justify the premium.

**Rules:**

- Never a percentile. Never "you rank in the Nth percentile."
- Never an average. Ranges only.
- State the sample size whenever specific figures are used — *"based on four comparable formats we have closed."*
- For categories outside F&B, Fashion and Retail, use E8 instead.

**[CHECK]** — these examples are structural placeholders. The real value comes from patterns you have actually observed. Each statement should be something a founder could not have worked out themselves and would not read anywhere else.

## E8. Insufficient Comparables

*Used where deal history does not support a category statement.*

> We have not yet closed enough deals in your category to offer a meaningful comparison, and we would rather say so than benchmark you against two or three businesses and present it as a pattern.
>
> Your assessment is unaffected — the seven pillars apply regardless of category. What is missing is the context of how businesses like yours typically compare, and we will have that as our deal history in this category grows.

**Why this is worth saying rather than silently omitting:** declining to publish a weak benchmark is itself evidence of how the benchmarks you *do* publish are constructed. A founder who reads this trusts the F&B numbers more, not less.

## Notes for the Team

**E3 and Group D must be read together.** The ranking formula is correct but counter-intuitive, and E3 is the only thing standing between a founder understanding it and assuming the report is broken. If either is edited, check the other still makes sense.

**E5 and E6 are a pair.** E5 states what is missing; E6 offers it. Separating them or reordering them turns an honest scope statement into a sales sequence. The order matters: state the limit first, then the resolution.

**E6 is the only piece in the library that sells anything.** Everything else diagnoses. The reason it works is that it arrives after E5 has already conceded the limitation — a reader who has just been told plainly what is missing is more receptive to being told where to get it than one who was pitched without the admission.

**E7 is where your consulting experience matters most in this group.** The examples given are structurally correct and substantively generic. A real category statement — something drawn from a pattern across actual closed deals — is the single hardest thing in this entire report for a competitor to replicate.
